COPD

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a progressive lung condition characterised by persistent airflow limitation. It affects 384 million people worldwide, with smoking responsible for 85–90% of cases.

What is COPD?

COPD is an umbrella term for a group of progressive lung diseases — primarily chronic bronchitis and emphysema — that cause obstructed airflow from the lungs. Unlike asthma, the airflow limitation in COPD is not fully reversible and tends to worsen over time.

In chronic bronchitis, the airways become inflamed and narrowed, producing excess mucus. In emphysema, the air sacs (alveoli) are gradually destroyed, reducing the surface area available for gas exchange. Most patients have a combination of both.

Causes & Risk Factors

  • Smoking: The leading cause, responsible for 85–90% of COPD cases
  • Long-term exposure to air pollutants, chemical fumes, or dust
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency: A genetic condition causing early-onset COPD
  • Recurrent childhood respiratory infections
  • Indoor air pollution (biomass fuel burning in low-income settings)

Symptoms

COPD develops slowly and symptoms may be attributed to "normal aging" for years:

  • Chronic cough, often producing mucus (sputum)
  • Shortness of breath, especially during physical activity
  • Wheezing
  • Chest tightness
  • Frequent respiratory infections
  • Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance

Diagnosis

COPD is diagnosed with spirometry, which measures the ratio of FEV1 to FVC (the amount of air exhaled in one second compared to total forced exhalation). A post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.70 confirms airflow limitation consistent with COPD.

COPD severity is then graded using the GOLD staging system (GOLD 1–4) based on the degree of airflow limitation.

Treatment

While COPD cannot be cured, treatment significantly improves quality of life and slows progression:

  • Smoking cessation: The single most effective intervention to slow disease progression
  • Bronchodilators (LAMA/LABA): Long-acting inhalers that open the airways
  • Inhaled corticosteroids: For patients with frequent exacerbations
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation: Exercise and education program that improves function and quality of life
  • Oxygen therapy: For patients with low blood oxygen levels
  • Vaccinations: Influenza and pneumococcal vaccines to prevent infections

Key Facts

  • 384 million people affected globally
  • 3rd leading cause of death worldwide
  • Smoking causes 85–90% of cases
  • Spirometry is required for diagnosis
